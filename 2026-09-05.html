✏️ Key Corrections

Tap a correction to open it.

1. Describe a busy month
Your wording“This September, I have many things. I have many things to do.”
Minimal correction“This September, I have many things to do.”
Natural version“I have a lot on my plate this September.”
Why? The first two sentences repeat the same idea. The minimal version removes the repetition, while “have a lot on my plate” is a natural conversational way to say that you are very busy.
2. Explain your responsibility
Your wording“I’m a person in charge—or personally charge—of my factory.”
Minimal correction“I’m the person in charge at my factory.”
Natural version“I’m responsible for greenhouse gas management at my factory.”
Why? “In charge” is a fixed phrase; “personally charge” cannot be used this way. The natural version names your exact responsibility and therefore sounds clearer and more professional.
3. Describe the reporting plan
Your wording“We make a plan how to calculate, how to verify greenhouse gas in my factory.”
Minimal correction“We are making a plan for how to calculate and verify greenhouse gas emissions at my factory.”
Natural version“We’re developing a process for calculating and verifying our factory’s greenhouse gas emissions.”
Why? Use “a plan for how to…” rather than “a plan how to,” and say “greenhouse gas emissions” when referring to the measured output. “Developing a process” better describes a formal workplace system.
4. Talk about graduate school
Your wording“When I was in D.C., I graduate from graduate school.”
Minimal correction“When I was in D.C., I graduated from graduate school.”
Natural version“I earned my graduate degree while I was living in Washington, D.C.”
Why? Graduation was completed in the past, so “graduate” must become “graduated.” “Earned my graduate degree” avoids repeating “graduate” and sounds smoother.
5. Explain your career move
Your wording“When return at my company, I asked my boss. I wanted to move to job related to greenhouse gas.”
Minimal correction“When I returned to my company, I asked my boss to move me to a job related to greenhouse gas management.”
Natural version“When I returned to Samsung, I asked to transfer into a sustainability-related role.”
Why? The clause needs the subject “I,” and the correct preposition is “return to.” In workplace English, “transfer into a role” is more precise than “move to job.”
6. Describe how long you have held the role
Your wording“Now I have worked in more than six months.”
Minimal correction“Now I have worked in this role for more than six months.”
Natural version“I’ve been in this role for more than six months now.”
Why? Use “for” with a duration, not “in.” Because the role began in the past and continues now, the present perfect form “I’ve been” is appropriate.
7. Talk about something you still miss
Your wording“I don’t have to wear a clean suit anymore, but sometimes I missed it.”
Minimal correction“I don’t have to wear a clean suit anymore, but sometimes I miss it.”
Natural version“I no longer have to wear a cleanroom suit, although I sometimes miss working on the factory floor.”
Why? “Sometimes” describes your current recurring feeling, so use the present tense “miss.” The natural version also clarifies that you miss the factory-floor work, not merely the clothing.
8. Describe an awkward relationship
Your wording“I feel a little bit awkward feeling to her.”
Minimal correction“I feel a little awkward with her.”
Natural version“I feel a little awkward around the person who previously handled this role.”
Why? After “feel,” use the adjective “awkward” directly; “awkward feeling” is unnecessary here. English normally uses “awkward with” or “awkward around” a person.
9. Distinguish “stressed” from “stressful”
Your wording“My mother was very stressful.”
Minimal correction“My mother was very stressed.”
Natural version“My mother was very worried that my brother might not meet the right person.”
Why? A person who experiences stress is “stressed.” Something that causes stress is “stressful.” “Worried” is even more precise because you explained what concerned your mother.
10. Keep the wedding story clear
Your wording“She didn’t choose his dress even a month before the wedding.”
Minimal correction“She hadn’t chosen her dress even a month before the wedding.”
Natural version“Even one month before the wedding, my brother’s fiancée still hadn’t chosen her wedding dress.”
Why? The dress belonged to your brother’s fiancée, so the correct pronoun is “her.” The past perfect “hadn’t chosen” shows that the choice was still unfinished at a specific point before the wedding.
